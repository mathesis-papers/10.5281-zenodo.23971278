Parity and product extensions of Bala's divisibilities (2026): OEIS A295462, References (record #19; contribution dated 23 August 2026)

Haobo Ma, Wenlin Zhang

Paper

Can the oddness of a number built from enormous factorials be read directly from the binary digits of its index? Haobo Ma and Wenlin Zhang answer yes for the ratio studied here: it is odd exactly when every two successive ones in that binary expansion have at least three zeros between them.

For a nonnegative integer n, the number is a(n) = (15n)!(2n)! / ((10n)!(6n)!n!), where a factorial multiplies all positive integers up to its argument, with 0! = 1. Such quotients need not generally be integers, so integrality comes before parity. The authors credit Bober’s classification of factorial ratios and Soundararajan’s later elementary treatment for that background. Their main advance concerns Peter Bala’s binary-spacing conjecture of 23 August 2026 for OEIS sequence A295462, originally entered by Gheorghe Coserea.

The proof counts how often each prime divides the quotient. This count, called its prime valuation, comes from counting multiples of the prime and its successive powers inside each factorial. At each scale, the calculation rounds down the five scaled factorial arguments and subtracts the denominator contributions. Because the numerator and denominator coefficient sums agree, the resulting step function repeats with period one. Checking its thirty equal subintervals shows that it takes only the values zero and one. Thus no prime remains in the reduced denominator, proving integrality; oddness means that every contribution for the prime two vanishes.

Binary spacing controls precisely these contributions. A one-bit in position e represents 2ᵉ. When all occupied positions are at least four apart, the total R below position e is smaller than 2ᵉ/15, by a geometric-series estimate. This keeps the rounding-down calculation in regions where its contribution is zero. Conversely, take the lowest pair of consecutive ones separated by only one, two or three positions. Everything below that pair already satisfies the spacing rule, so the same bound controls the remaining digits; the pair forces one contribution to equal one. Other contributions cannot cancel it. For example, 17 has binary expansion 10001 and gives an odd value, whereas 9, written 1001, gives an even value.

The authors also reprove Bala’s divisibilities by 6n+1 and 10n+1. These factors are odd and coprime, so their product divides a(n), and dividing by it preserves the parity criterion. The admissible indices form the existing sequence A048718. If cₘ counts admissible length-m binary words, then cₘ = cₘ₋₁ + cₘ₋₄ for m ≥ 4: a word starts either with zero or with 1000. The initial counts are 1, 2, 3, 4. Spacing also makes multiplication by numbers below sixteen act in disjoint four-bit blocks, explaining identities involving bitwise exclusive-or, which retains positions occupied in exactly one input. The paper gives written alternative proofs of eleven identities proposed by Paul D. Hanna and previously verified by Sebastian Karlsson using Walnut; the two longer-mask cases additionally require controlling carries.

A second ratio, T(n) = (12n)!n! / ((6n)!(4n)!(3n)!), leads to Bala’s divisibility questions dated 28 August 2025. The authors establish that n+1 divides 385T(n), 2n+1 divides 5T(n), and 3n+1 divides T(n). They also prove that their three-factor product divides 770T(n), and that 12n−1 divides T(n). These assert integrality, not positivity: at n = 0 the last quotient is negative. Here a related step function has twelve intervals; base-two and base-three digit sums handle the small primes separately.

Let L(r) be the least common multiple of the integers from one through r. For R ≥ 1 and k = 1, 2 or 3, multiplying T(n) by 36(R−1)!L(12R) makes it divisible by the product of kn+i over 1 ≤ i ≤ R. Multiplying by (R−1)!L(R) handles the product of 12n−i over indices in that range coprime to twelve. For either product, its prime valuation is bounded by its largest individual valuation plus that of (R−1)!; subtracting factors reduces the remaining estimates to index differences. The step function supplies sufficiently high prime powers, while the least common multiples absorb lower-level exceptions.

These are all-input written proofs, not conclusions inferred from testing. The authors describe a pinned Lean declaration covering only integrality and parity of a(n), not the corollaries. Separately, they report finite checks of binary parity below 2¹⁶ and arithmetic through n = 256, with product-family checks restricted to R ≤ 32. The significance is the connection between factorial divisibility and sparse binary digits, alongside explicit constants for the two product families. The paper does not settle moment-density questions or analogous divisibilities for the other fifty-one sporadic ratios; separate congruence and fractional-argument results are credited to earlier work, including Bajaj–Burns and Blanco Gómez.
